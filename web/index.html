A question in geometry

The Shortest Path Through a Curved World

How heat reveals the shortest route across a complex 3D surface.

You are standing somewhere on a curved world. A beacon is waiting on the other side. What is the shortest path home?

Follow the question ↓

I · The tempting answer

A straight line cheats.

In flat space, the shortest path between two points is a straight line. Here that line cuts through the planet. The explorer cannot follow it without leaving the surface.

Through ambient 3D space Constrained to the surface

The constraint changes the geometry—and therefore changes “shortest.”

II · What the computer sees

A smooth world, made of triangles.

A computer does not see a perfectly smooth planet. It sees 10,242 vertices joined into 20,480 oriented triangular faces. Each triangle is flat; together they approximate the folds, ridges, and valleys of the surface.

One quick baseline is to walk only along mesh edges. Dijkstra's algorithm finds the shortest such graph route, but its turns inherit the triangulation. Refine or rotate the mesh and that jagged route changes.

An edge route is intrinsic to the graph—not the exact continuous surface.

III · The central experiment

Release heat.

Imagine the beacon as a warm point on a cold world. Heat reaches nearby places first, then bends around hills and flows through passages because it is constrained to the same surface as the explorer.

Could the way heat spreads reveal distance?

Six diffusion states, solved by the C++ engine.

IV · Heat becomes direction

Read the slope of warmth.

At every triangle, the heat values form a tiny slope. Its gradient points toward the fastest increase. Reverse and normalize that slope, and the mesh gains a clean unit direction field.

Follow the reconstructed distance downhill across triangle faces and a surface-constrained route reaches the beacon. This path is called a geodesic.

V · The mathematics

Three equations, one idea.

The implementation uses a positive-semidefinite cotangent stiffness matrix . With that sign convention, backward Euler diffusion is stable and the same sparse operators work on every query.

1

Diffuse briefly. stores vertex area, measures surface variation, ties diffusion time to mean edge length, and places a unit source at the beacon. The solution is heat.

2

Keep direction, discard magnitude. Each face has a constant piecewise-linear gradient. points approximately in the direction in which distance increases.

3

Integrate the directions. The Poisson solve finds the scalar field whose gradient best matches . Here is the weak gradient load—the negative integrated continuum divergence for our convention. Subtract the source value and becomes distance.

VI · Computation at scale

Geometry becomes sparse algebra.

On a dense mesh, most vertices interact only with their one-ring neighbors. That locality creates sparse matrices: millions of possible entries, but only a small band of real work. Operator assembly and factorization are preprocessing; each new source reuses them.

The answer

Heat can reveal distance.

Let heat move for an instant. Read the direction in which it changes. Reconstruct the distance field that agrees with those directions. Then follow that field home.

For the technically curious

Under the hood

The story above is backed by a C++20 geometry engine, not a radial shader. This section separates what is exact, what is approximate, and what was actually measured.

01

Mesh representation

An oriented halfedge structure stores vertices, faces, edges, twins, next links, vertex incidence, cached one-rings, and boundary state. Construction rejects non-manifold orientation and can explicitly reject or skip degenerate triangles.

02

Discrete operators

Barycentric vertex areas form a lumped mass matrix. Per-triangle cotangents assemble a symmetric stiffness matrix; face barycentric gradients provide scalar gradient and weak divergence loads. Closed and boundary meshes use natural Neumann conditions.

03

Numerical design

Eigen's SimplicialLDLT supplies the reference direct path; preconditioned conjugate gradient supplies an iterative path. Both report true relative residuals. Heat and pinned Poisson factorizations are reused between source queries.

04

Path extraction

The route follows the piecewise-linear distance gradient within a face, computes the next barycentric edge crossing, transfers to the adjacent face, and stops inside a source neighborhood. Cycle, critical-point, boundary, and maximum-step guards provide a monotone vertex fallback.

05

CUDA architecture

The optional backend keeps CSR matrices and work vectors resident, uses cuSPARSE for sparse matrix-vector products, cuBLAS reductions, a Jacobi-preconditioned conjugate gradient loop, and custom geometry, update, and normalization kernels. Double precision is deliberate for CPU agreement.

06

Correctness

Tests cover topology, boundary traversal, areas and normals, Laplacian symmetry and row sums, positive mass, constant gradients, solve residuals, deterministic exports, path termination, a flat domain, great-circle distance, solver agreement, and malformed input.

Limitations

The Heat Method approximates geodesic distance and converges under refinement; it is not an exact polyhedral-geodesic algorithm. Cotangent quality depends on triangle shape. Extracted paths inherit errors in the scalar field and may need a guarded fallback near critical points. The edge-Dijkstra baseline is graph distance, not a continuous geodesic. CUDA code is compile-covered in CI but was not runtime-measured on this Mac.
